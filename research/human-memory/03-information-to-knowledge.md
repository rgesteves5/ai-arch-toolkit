# From Information to Knowledge: Categories and Transformations

## Overview

Information and knowledge are fundamentally different things. **Information** is raw data — facts, figures, observations. **Knowledge** is information that has been processed, organized, connected, and made meaningful. The journey from one to the other involves several cognitive transformations and the integration of new information into existing mental structures.

---

## The DIKW Hierarchy

One classic framework describes the progression from raw data to wisdom as a pyramid with four levels:

### Data

- Raw, unprocessed symbols — numbers, words, signals, measurements
- Has no inherent meaning on its own
- Example: The number "37" is data — it could mean anything without context
- In computing: bits, bytes, raw sensor readings, unstructured text

### Information

- Data that has been organized, structured, and given context
- Answers questions like "who," "what," "where," "when"
- Example: "The patient's body temperature is 37°C" is information — you now know what the number refers to
- Information is data with relevance and purpose
- In computing: structured database records, formatted reports, labeled datasets

### Knowledge

- Information that has been integrated with understanding, experience, reasoning, and connection to other knowledge
- Answers questions like "how" and "why"
- Example: A doctor who knows that 37°C is normal body temperature, understands thermoregulation, recognizes when temperature readings are clinically significant, and can interpret the reading in the context of the patient's symptoms has knowledge
- Knowledge involves the ability to use information — to make inferences, draw conclusions, and take appropriate action
- Knowledge is deeply personal and contextual — two people with the same information may have different knowledge because of their different experiences and existing knowledge structures

### Wisdom

- The apex of the pyramid
- The ability to apply knowledge with judgment, ethics, and insight in novel and complex situations
- Knowing when and how to act on what you know — and when not to
- Involves meta-knowledge (knowing what you don't know), ethical reasoning, and the ability to navigate ambiguity
- Example: A wise doctor not only knows the medical facts but also considers the patient's values, the uncertainty of the diagnosis, the trade-offs between treatments, and the broader context of the patient's life
- Wisdom is the rarest and most difficult level to achieve

## How the Brain Transforms Information into Knowledge

### Schema Assimilation and Accommodation

When new information enters your cognitive system, it initially exists as an isolated representation. Through elaboration, practice, and connection-building, it becomes integrated into your existing **schema** — a mental framework or model of how things relate to each other.

The psychologist Jean Piaget described two key processes:

**Assimilation:**
- New information fits neatly into an existing schema
- The new information is interpreted through the lens of what you already know
- Example: A biology student learning about a new organ system that follows familiar principles of structure and function
- Assimilation is relatively effortless — it extends existing understanding without requiring fundamental restructuring
- Risk: Assimilation can distort new information to fit existing schemas, leading to misconceptions

**Accommodation:**
- New information doesn't fit existing schemas and forces restructuring
- Your mental models must be revised, expanded, or fundamentally changed
- Example: A physics student moving from Newtonian mechanics to quantum mechanics — the new framework requires fundamentally different ways of thinking about causality, determinism, and observation
- Accommodation is more difficult and often uncomfortable — it involves cognitive conflict (Piaget called this **disequilibrium**)
- But accommodation leads to deeper, more accurate understanding

**Equilibration:**
- The ongoing process of balancing assimilation and accommodation
- Piaget proposed that cognitive development is driven by the constant cycle of encountering information that doesn't fit (disequilibrium) and restructuring schemas to accommodate it (re-equilibration)

### From Controlled to Automatic Processing

As knowledge becomes deeply integrated, retrieval becomes more automatic and requires less conscious effort.

- **Controlled processing**: Slow, effortful, conscious, serial (one step at a time), limited by working memory capacity. Characteristic of novices.
- **Automatic processing**: Fast, effortless, unconscious, parallel, not limited by working memory. Characteristic of experts.

An expert chess player doesn't think through every possible move — they recognize patterns instantly because their knowledge is richly organized and highly accessible. A fluent reader doesn't sound out each letter — word recognition has become automatic.

This transition from controlled to automatic processing is a hallmark of expertise and reflects the gradual shift from hippocampus-dependent explicit memory to basal ganglia-mediated procedural memory and cortically distributed pattern recognition.

### Chunking and Knowledge Compilation

**Chunking** is the process of organizing individual elements into larger, meaningful units.

- A novice chess player sees individual pieces; a grandmaster sees familiar configurations (chunks) of pieces
- A beginning reader processes individual letters; a fluent reader processes whole words or phrases as single units
- An expert programmer sees design patterns where a beginner sees individual lines of code
- Herbert Simon estimated that expertise in a domain requires acquiring roughly 50,000–100,000 chunks

**Knowledge compilation** (John Anderson's ACT theory) describes how declarative knowledge ("knowing that") is gradually converted into procedural knowledge ("knowing how") through practice. Initially, you follow explicit rules; eventually, the process becomes a single, fast, automatic procedure.

## Categories of Knowledge

### By Source

#### Empirical Knowledge
- Comes from observation and experience — what you learn through your senses and experiments
- The foundation of scientific knowledge
- Requires careful observation, measurement, and often controlled experimentation
- Example: Knowing that water freezes at 0°C because you've observed it or because scientists have measured it
- Strengths: Grounded in reality, testable, verifiable
- Limitations: Limited by the scope of observation, subject to perceptual biases

#### Rational Knowledge
- Comes from logical reasoning, deduction, and abstract thought
- Independent of sensory experience (though often applied to empirical observations)
- Example: Mathematical proofs, logical deductions, philosophical arguments
- A priori knowledge — knowable through reason alone (e.g., "all bachelors are unmarried")
- Strengths: Certain (if the premises and logic are valid), universal
- Limitations: Requires valid premises; logic alone cannot tell you about the empirical world

#### Testimonial Knowledge
- Comes from what others tell you — teachers, books, media, conversations, culture
- The vast majority of what any individual knows falls into this category
- We rely on chains of testimony — your teacher learned from their teacher, who learned from books, which were written by researchers, and so on
- Requires trust and the evaluation of source credibility
- Example: Most of your historical knowledge, scientific knowledge, and geographic knowledge comes from testimony rather than direct experience
- Critical thinking, source evaluation, and media literacy are essential skills for managing testimonial knowledge

#### Intuitive Knowledge
- A sense of knowing that is difficult to articulate or explain
- Gut feelings, hunches, instinctive responses
- Often arises from **pattern recognition below conscious awareness** — your brain has detected a pattern from extensive experience, but the recognition process is implicit
- Expert intuition (as studied by Gary Klein in naturalistic decision-making) can be highly reliable in domains with regular patterns and feedback
- But intuition can also be unreliable, especially in unfamiliar domains or when influenced by cognitive biases
- Example: An experienced firefighter who "feels" that a building is about to collapse, based on subtle cues processed unconsciously

### By Accessibility

#### Explicit Knowledge (Codified / Declarative)
- Can be written down, spoken, diagrammed, and easily transferred to others
- Formal, systematic, and articulable
- Examples: textbook facts, mathematical formulas, standard operating procedures, recipes, maps, dictionaries
- Easily stored in databases, books, and documents
- Can be taught through direct instruction
- The kind of knowledge that formal education focuses on

#### Tacit Knowledge
- Difficult or impossible to articulate or transfer through words alone
- The kind of knowing that comes from experience, practice, and immersion
- Michael Polanyi's famous statement: **"We know more than we can tell."**
- Examples:
  - A master craftsperson's "feel" for their material — knowing exactly how much pressure to apply, when the clay is ready, how the grain of the wood will behave
  - A therapist's instinct for when a patient is holding something back
  - A surgeon's hand movements, refined over thousands of operations
  - Native speaker's grammatical intuitions — you know a sentence "sounds wrong" without being able to cite the rule
- Can only be transferred through apprenticeship, mentorship, shared practice, and extended experience
- Deeply tied to procedural and implicit memory systems
- Often undervalued in formal education but critical for professional expertise
- The distinction between explicit and tacit knowledge has major implications for knowledge management in organizations — you can't simply document tacit knowledge and expect it to transfer

### By Structure

#### Declarative Knowledge — "Knowing That"
- Facts, concepts, propositions, and relationships
- Can be stated or declared
- Examples: "Paris is the capital of France," "Water is H₂O," "DNA encodes genetic information"
- Subdivides into:
  - **Factual knowledge**: Discrete, specific pieces of information — names, dates, definitions, terminology, specific details
  - **Conceptual knowledge**: Understanding of categories, principles, theories, models, and the relationships between them — grasping *why* things work the way they do, not just *what* they are

#### Procedural Knowledge — "Knowing How"
- Skills, techniques, methods, and algorithms
- Knowledge of how to perform actions and solve problems
- Examples: How to solve a quadratic equation, how to drive a car, how to write a persuasive essay, how to conduct a scientific experiment
- Often begins as declarative knowledge (following explicit rules) and becomes procedural through practice (knowledge compilation)
- Tested by performance rather than verbal recall

#### Conditional Knowledge — "Knowing When and Why"
- Understanding the conditions under which certain facts apply or certain strategies should be used
- Often what separates novices from experts
- A novice might know several problem-solving techniques (procedural knowledge); an expert knows **which one to deploy in which situation** (conditional knowledge)
- Examples:
  - Knowing not just how to perform CPR but when it's appropriate and when it's not
  - Knowing not just several statistical tests but which one to use for which type of data and research question
  - Knowing not just how to use different cooking techniques but which technique brings out the best in each ingredient
- Develops through extensive experience with varied situations and feedback

#### Metacognitive Knowledge — "Knowing About Your Own Knowing"
- Understanding your own cognitive processes, strengths, weaknesses, and strategies
- Knowing what you know and what you don't know
- Knowing which learning strategies work best for you
- Being able to monitor your own comprehension in real time — recognizing when you don't understand something
- Critical for self-directed learning and academic success
- Components:
  - **Metacognitive knowledge**: Understanding of your own cognition (e.g., "I learn better with visual aids")
  - **Metacognitive regulation**: Planning, monitoring, and evaluating your cognitive processes (e.g., adjusting study strategies based on self-assessment)
  - **Metacognitive experiences**: Feelings of knowing, tip-of-the-tongue states, feelings of difficulty
- Can be taught and improved — teaching students metacognitive strategies is one of the most effective educational interventions

### By Domain and Abstraction

#### Domain-Specific Knowledge
- Tied to a particular field, discipline, or area of expertise
- Includes the specialized vocabulary, concepts, methods, standards of evidence, and ways of thinking characteristic of that field
- Examples: medical knowledge, legal knowledge, musical knowledge, programming knowledge, culinary knowledge
- Deep domain-specific knowledge is essential for expertise — general intelligence alone is not sufficient
- Expert performance depends heavily on the quantity and organization of domain-specific knowledge

#### Domain-General Knowledge
- Transfers across fields and disciplines
- Includes critical thinking skills, reading comprehension, logical reasoning, general problem-solving heuristics, communication skills, and metacognitive strategies
- Example: The ability to evaluate evidence, construct arguments, identify assumptions, and detect logical fallacies applies across all domains
- There is ongoing debate in education and cognitive science about the relative importance of domain-general vs. domain-specific knowledge
- Current consensus: Both are important, but domain-specific knowledge is often undervalued; you cannot think critically in a vacuum — critical thinking requires knowledge to think critically *about*

#### Conceptual Knowledge
- Understanding of principles, theories, models, and relationships
- Grasping *why* things work the way they do, not just what they are
- Enables transfer — if you understand the underlying principle, you can apply it to new situations
- Example: Understanding conservation of energy lets you analyze novel physical systems you've never encountered before
- Often described as "deep" understanding as opposed to "surface" knowledge of facts

#### Factual Knowledge
- Discrete pieces of information — names, dates, definitions, terminology, specific data points
- Often dismissed as "mere facts" or "rote memorization," but factual knowledge is essential
- Facts serve as the building blocks that conceptual knowledge organizes and connects
- You cannot think critically about a topic you know nothing about
- The key is that factual knowledge should be organized within conceptual frameworks, not learned in isolation

## The Journey from Information to Knowledge: A Summary

The complete transformation looks like this:

1. **Perception**: You encounter data through your senses (sensory memory)
2. **Attention**: You select relevant information for further processing (working memory's central executive)
3. **Encoding**: You process the information deeply — connecting it to existing knowledge, elaborating on its meaning, generating examples (working memory → long-term memory)
4. **Consolidation**: During rest and sleep, the hippocampus replays and gradually transfers the new information to neocortical networks, integrating it with existing knowledge structures
5. **Retrieval and Application**: You recall and use the knowledge in various contexts, each retrieval strengthening the memory and building new connections
6. **Reorganization**: With accumulating experience, your knowledge structures (schemas) are refined, reorganized, and made more efficient — moving from fragmented facts toward integrated understanding
7. **Automatization**: Frequently used knowledge becomes automatic, freeing up working memory for more complex processing

**The most durable and useful knowledge is knowledge that is:**
- **Richly connected** — linked to many other things you know
- **Deeply processed** — understood rather than merely memorized
- **Frequently retrieved** — practiced and applied across varied contexts
- **Emotionally meaningful** — tied to personal relevance, curiosity, or significance
- **Well-organized** — structured within coherent schemas and conceptual frameworks
- **Flexibly accessible** — retrievable from multiple cues and applicable to novel situations
